# 5.3. Orbital Energy and Cosmic Velocities

> [!abstract] In brief
> Specific mechanical energy in a Newtonian field is ε = v²/2 − μ/r, where μ = GM. A bound ellipse has ε < 0 and ε = −μ/(2a); zero energy is the parabolic escape boundary and positive energy gives a hyperbola.

## Core explanation

Circular speed at radius r is √(μ/r), while no-further-thrust escape speed is √(2μ/r). A brief impulse changes velocity but not instantaneous position; its direction sets new energy, angular momentum, and the opposite apsis.

## Key ideas

- **Circular and elliptical orbits.** — A circular orbit has constant radius and speed, while an ellipse exchanges kinetic and potential energy at fixed total. The vis-viva equation v² = μ(2/r − 1/a) covers both.
- **The first and second cosmic velocities: circular-orbit speed and escape speed.** — First cosmic speed in idealized usage is circular speed at the surface, and second cosmic speed is surface escape speed. They depend on the planet and are not universal constants.
- **Bound and unbound trajectories.** — Negative ε gives a finite apoapsis, ε = 0 reaches infinity with zero residual speed, and ε > 0 retains excess speed. The actual path may intersect the surface first.
- **Changing an orbit with a brief impulse.** — A prograde impulse on a circular orbit normally raises apoapsis and leaves the burn point as new periapsis; a retrograde impulse lowers the opposite side. A radial impulse also rotates the ellipse.

## Short example

- **Problem:** Estimate both speeds at Earth radius R = 6.371×10⁶ m with μ = 3.986×10¹⁴ m³/s², ignoring atmosphere and rotation.
- **Answer:** Ideal surface values are about 7.91 km/s circular speed and 11.19 km/s escape speed.
- **Check:** The ratio v_esc/v_c = √2 ≈ 1.414 is independent of M and r at the same point in a Newtonian field.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Surface cosmic speeds omit atmosphere, rotation, terrain, and finite burn time; one-μ equations assume a spherical central body with no important perturbations.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> A speed below escape speed does not guarantee an orbit: an unsuitable direction may make the path intersect the planet, and an atmosphere dissipates energy.

## Self-check

1. What happens to the far side of a circular orbit after a brief prograde impulse?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> At the burn point energy and tangential speed rise while position has not changed yet.
> The burn point becomes periapsis of a new ellipse and the opposite apoapsis rises, provided escape speed is not reached.

2. Find circular speed at r = 7.0×10⁶ m from Earth's center for μ = 3.986×10¹⁴ m³/s².

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Use v_c = √(μ/r).
> v_c = √(3.986×10¹⁴ / 7.0×10⁶) ≈ 7546 m/s ≈ 7.55 km/s.
