# 3.5. Normal Force, Tension, and Elasticity

> [!abstract] In brief
> A support, string, or spring restricts possible motion and develops force through contact or deformation. Its magnitude is normally found from equations of motion together with geometrical constraints rather than assigned in advance.

## Core explanation

Everyday apparent weight often means the scale reading, a normal force. It equals mg only without vertical acceleration; an accelerating elevator changes the reading while gravitational force mg remains nearly constant.

## Key ideas

- **The force of gravity and apparent weight.** — Gravitational force is the field's action on a body. A support scale reads contact force and responds to acceleration, so it is useful to distinguish mg from apparent weight N.
- **The normal force.** — Normal force is perpendicular to a surface and takes the value required by the no-penetration constraint while contact persists. It need not equal mg.
- **Tension in a string.** — An ideal massless inextensible string transmits equal tension along a segment when pulleys are ideal. A massive string or pulley can have different tensions.
- **Hooke's law.** — Within its linear elastic range, a spring exerts F = −kx relative to equilibrium. The constant k depends on material and geometry, and Hooke's law fails beyond the elastic regime.
- **Systems of connected bodies and pulleys.** — For connected bodies, write a separate second law and one constraint such as equal acceleration magnitudes for ends of an inextensible string. Internal tension disappears only when balancing the whole system.
- **Apparent weight in an accelerating elevator.** — For upward elevator acceleration N = m(g + a); with +y upward, downward acceleration has negative a. During free fall the contact reading approaches zero.

## Short example

- **Problem:** A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What force does the scale read for g = 9.81 m/s²?
- **Answer:** The scale measures about 827 N, corresponding to about 84.3 kg on a mass-calibrated display that divides force by g.
- **Check:** For upward acceleration N > mg ≈ 687 N, consistent with greater apparent weight.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Equal tension requires a massless string and ideal pulley, while Hooke's law requires small reversible deformation; when contact is lost, normal force becomes zero and the former constraint equation no longer applies.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Do not write N = mg before analyzing acceleration and other vertical forces; it is a consequence of a particular balance, not the definition of normal force.

## Formulas

- [[../Formula Index#Newton's Second Law|Newton's Second Law]] — ΣF = dp/dt; for constant mass, ΣF = ma

## Self-check

1. Can normal force on a block resting on an incline be less than mg even without vertical free fall?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> The support balances the component perpendicular to the plane, not necessarily all of gravity.
> Yes. With no other perpendicular forces, N = mg cos θ, which is less than mg for a nonzero incline angle.

2. An ideal Atwood machine has masses 3.0 and 2.0 kg. Find acceleration magnitude for g = 9.81 m/s².

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> For the full system, driving force is the weight difference and inertial mass is the sum.
> a = (3−2)g/(3+2) = 1.96 m/s², with the 3 kg mass moving down.
