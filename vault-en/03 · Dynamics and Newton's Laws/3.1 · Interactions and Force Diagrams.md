# 3.1. Interactions and Force Diagrams

> [!abstract] In brief
> Force is not something stored inside a body; it is a vector description of a particular interaction. Before applying the second law, select a system, list external objects that interact with it, and draw one force for each interaction on a free-body diagram.

## Core explanation

A good diagram separates geometry from dynamics. It shows chosen axes and forces, but not velocity, acceleration, an assumed direction of motion, or forces exerted on other bodies; each force is then projected onto the axes.

## Key ideas

- **Force as a description of an interaction.** — Gravitational, electromagnetic, and contact forces describe one object's effect on another. A force label should identify both the source and the body receiving it.
- **Choosing the system under study.** — The system boundary decides which forces are external. If two connected blocks are treated together, tension between them is internal; for one block, the same tension is external.
- **External and internal influences.** — Internal forces can change relative motion of parts but cancel pairwise from the total momentum balance of a closed system. Interaction with objects beyond the boundary supplies external influence.
- **The free-body diagram.** — A separate sketch isolates the selected body and shows force arrows, including points or lines of action when torque matters. The diagram turns a verbal problem into a vector equation.
- **Typical spurious or nonexistent forces introduced in incorrect free-body diagrams.** — Velocity and acceleration are not forces, and an inertial force appears only after an explicit choice of a non-inertial frame. The third-law partner acts on another body and does not belong on this diagram.

## Short example

- **Problem:** A 5.0 kg block is pulled horizontally with 18 N while kinetic friction is 3 N. Draw the force balance and find acceleration for g = 9.81 m/s².
- **Answer:** The block accelerates at 3.0 m/s² to the right; vertical forces cancel.
- **Check:** Acceleration follows the resultant, not necessarily velocity, and N/kg is equivalent to m/s².

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> A force diagram depends on both system boundary and reference frame; a non-inertial frame needs explicitly labeled inertial forces to retain the form ma = ΣF, while an extended body may also require torques and distributed loads.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Do not draw both the support force on the block and the block's force on the support on one block diagram: they are a third-law pair acting on different bodies.

## Formulas

- [[../Formula Index#Newton's Second Law|Newton's Second Law]] — ΣF = dp/dt; for constant mass, ΣF = ma

## Self-check

1. Which forces belong on the diagram of a book resting on a table?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Name an external source for every arrow.
> Earth's gravitational force downward and the table's normal force upward. Their equality follows from zero acceleration, not from Newton's third law.

2. A 2 kg body experiences 7 N right and 3 N left. Find its acceleration.

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Find the vector sum of forces first.
> The resultant is 4 N right, so a = 4/2 = 2 m/s² right.
