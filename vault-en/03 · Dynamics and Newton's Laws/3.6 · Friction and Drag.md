# 3.6. Friction and Drag

> [!abstract] In brief
> Friction and drag emerge from many microscopic interactions and are described by effective models. Static friction adjusts up to a limiting value, while kinetic friction is often approximated as a constant fraction of normal force.

## Core explanation

Fluid resistance opposes velocity relative to the medium and depends on flow regime. It can be approximately linear at low Reynolds number and quadratic in inertia-dominated flow; terminal speed occurs when drag balances the driving force.

## Key ideas

- **Static and kinetic friction.** — At rest |f_s| ≤ μ_sN: friction takes the value needed up to its maximum. After sliding begins, the simple model gives |f_k| ≈ μ_kN opposite relative motion, commonly with μ_k < μ_s.
- **The coefficient of friction and the limits of applicability of the simple model.** — Coefficients depend on material pair, contamination, temperature, and contact regime. The μN equation is not a fundamental law and poorly represents lubrication, rolling, or adhesive contacts.
- **Drag proportional to speed and to the square of speed.** — Linear drag F_d = −bv describes, for example, slow viscous flow around a small sphere. Quadratic drag F_d = −½ρC_dAv|v| is more common at high Reynolds number.
- **Terminal velocity.** — Terminal speed occurs when the force sum is zero and acceleration vanishes. For a falling body with quadratic drag, v_t = √(2mg/(ρC_dA)) if parameters stay constant.
- **A falling skydiver and a moving car.** — A skydiver's parachute increases area and changes C_d, sharply reducing terminal speed. For a car in a quadratic regime, aerodynamic power demand grows approximately as v³.

## Short example

- **Problem:** An 80 kg body falls while upward drag is modeled as kv² with k = 0.25 kg/m. Find terminal speed and downward acceleration at v = 40 m/s, taking g = 9.81 m/s².
- **Answer:** Terminal speed is about 56.0 m/s; at 40 m/s the body still accelerates downward at 4.81 m/s².
- **Check:** At v = 0 acceleration is g, while at v = v_t it is zero, matching the limiting cases.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Dry-friction and drag coefficients are empirical and may vary with speed, roughness, orientation, and flow regime; no universal sharp boundary separates linear and quadratic drag laws.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Static friction is not always μ_sN; that is only its upper bound, and friction takes a smaller value whenever that is sufficient to prevent sliding.

## Self-check

1. A 10 kg block on a horizontal surface is pushed with 20 N and μ_s = 0.40. It starts at rest. What is friction?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Compare the required 20 N with μ_smg.
> The limit is about 0.40×10×9.81 = 39.2 N, so the block remains at rest and static friction is 20 N opposite the push.

2. With quadratic drag, body area is quadrupled without changing C_d, ρ, or mass. How does terminal speed change?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Area appears under a square root in the denominator of v_t.
> Terminal speed decreases by √4 = 2.
