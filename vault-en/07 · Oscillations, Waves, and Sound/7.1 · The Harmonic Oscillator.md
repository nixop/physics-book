# 7.1. The Harmonic Oscillator

> [!abstract] In brief
> At stable equilibrium, a small displacement produces a force directed back toward equilibrium. If potential energy is smooth, its first nonzero term near a minimum is usually quadratic, making the restoring force linear in displacement. The equation $m \ddot{x} + kx = 0$ then has sinusoidal solutions.

## Core explanation

Amplitude and initial phase are fixed by the initial state, while the natural angular frequency $\omega_{0} = \sqrt{k/m}$ is fixed by system parameters. The same mathematical form describes a spring, a small-angle pendulum, and many electrical or acoustic systems, but only while nonlinearity and loss remain negligible.

## Key ideas

- **Stable equilibrium.** — Stable equilibrium corresponds to a local minimum of potential energy: after a small displacement, the system accelerates back toward the equilibrium point.
- **Amplitude, period, frequency, and phase.** — Amplitude is the maximum displacement, period T is the repeat time, $f = 1/T$, $\omega = 2 \pi f$, and phase specifies the location within a cycle.
- **A spring oscillator is harmonic while Hooke's law applies.** — For an ideal spring $F = -kx$, so mass and stiffness determine $\omega_0$; leaving the linear Hooke-law range creates harmonics and an amplitude-dependent period.
- **A simple pendulum becomes harmonic only in the small-angle approximation `sin θ ≈ θ`, with `θ` expressed in radians.** — The exact pendulum equation contains $\sin\theta$; replacing it by $\theta$ is valid only for a small angle expressed in radians and makes the equation linear.
- **Harmonic motion as the projection of uniform circular motion.** — A point moving uniformly around a circle has coordinate $A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)$ on a selected axis, geometrically explaining the sine and cosine solution.

## Short example

- **Problem:** A 0.50 kg mass is attached to a spring of stiffness 200 N/m. Neglect loss. Find its natural angular frequency, period, and frequency.
- **Answer:** $\omega_{0} = 20\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, $T = 0.314\,\mathrm{s}$, and $f = 3.18\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
- **Check:** $k/m$ has units of 1/s^2; increasing mass must reduce frequency, as the square root predicts.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> The ideal harmonic-oscillator model requires a linear restoring force, constant parameters, and negligible loss; large pendulum angles, a nonlinear spring, or dry friction change both waveform and period.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> A small angle is not merely a small number of degrees: $\theta$ must be in radians when using $\sin\theta$ approximately equal to $\theta$.

## Self-check

1. Does the period of an ideal spring oscillator change if amplitude is doubled within the linear range?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Check whether A appears in $\omega_{0} = \sqrt{k/m}$.
> No. The ideal linear period is amplitude-independent; a measured dependence indicates nonlinearity or another violated assumption.

2. By what factor does period change if mass is quadrupled while the spring is unchanged?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> T is proportional to $\sqrt{m/k}$.
> The period increases by $\sqrt{4} = 2$.
