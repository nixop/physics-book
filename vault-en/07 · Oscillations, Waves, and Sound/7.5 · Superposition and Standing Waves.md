# 7.5. Superposition and Standing Waves

> [!abstract] In brief
> In a linear medium, the sum of two wave-equation solutions is also a solution. Displacements therefore add with their signs and phases: equal phases reinforce, while opposite phases can cancel. Interference redistributes amplitude in space but does not destroy the energy of the entire closed system.

## Core explanation

Two opposing harmonic waves of equal frequency and amplitude form a standing wave. Its nodes remain fixed while antinodes oscillate between them. Boundary conditions admit only wavelengths that fit the system geometry, so a string, air column, or resonator has discrete normal modes.

## Key ideas

- **The superposition of waves.** — Superposition applies when the medium response is linear: total displacement is the algebraic sum of separate displacements at the same point and time.
- **Constructive and destructive interference.** — Constructive and destructive behavior is set by phase difference; complete cancellation requires equal amplitudes and opposite phases at the point considered.
- **Standing waves, nodes, and antinodes.** — A standing wave can have zero average energy flux although each segment between nodes exchanges kinetic and potential energy.
- **Boundary conditions and normal modes.** — A fixed string end requires zero displacement, so L = n lambda/2 and f_n = n v/(2L); another boundary type gives a different mode set.

## Short example

- **Problem:** A 0.65 m string is fixed at both ends, held at tension 80 N, and has linear density 5.0e-3 kg/m. Find its first and third natural frequencies.
- **Answer:** f1 is about 97 Hz and f3 is about 292 Hz.
- **Check:** Three half-wavelengths fit along the string in the third mode; increasing tension raises every frequency as sqrt(T).

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Linear superposition and ideal-string mode formulas require small transverse displacement, constant tension and linear density, fixed ideal boundaries, and weak loss; string stiffness and compliant supports shift the frequencies.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Destructive interference at one point does not mean energy vanished: it remains elsewhere or in another form if no absorption occurs.

## Formulas

- [[../Formula Index#Natural Frequencies of a Stretched String|Natural Frequencies of a Stretched String]] — f_n = n/(2L) · sqrt(T/μ), n = 1, 2, 3, …

## Self-check

1. Why is a fixed string end a node?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> What kinematic boundary condition does the support impose?
> The support forbids transverse displacement, so incident and reflected waves must always sum to zero there.

2. How does f1 change if ideal-string tension is increased by a factor of 9?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> f1 is proportional to sqrt(T).
> The fundamental frequency increases by a factor of 3.
