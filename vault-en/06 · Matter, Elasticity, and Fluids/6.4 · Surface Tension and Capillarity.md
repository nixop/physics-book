# 6.4. Surface Tension and Capillarity

> [!abstract] In brief
> A molecule inside a liquid is surrounded on all sides, whereas the environment of a surface molecule is asymmetric. Creating a new interface requires work, so at fixed conditions the free surface energy changes as dW = gamma dA. The same coefficient gamma describes tangential force per unit length along a line in the interface.

## Core explanation

Surface curvature creates a pressure jump, and the competition among surface energy, gravity, and contact with a solid determines shape. In a narrow capillary, the vertical component of surface tension can support a liquid column. The effect strengthens rapidly as size decreases because surface force scales with length while weight scales with volume.

## Key ideas

- **Surface energy.** — The coefficient gamma is reversible work per unit increase of area at specified temperature and composition; contaminants and temperature can change it substantially.
- **The shape of a droplet.** — Without gravity, a droplet of fixed volume approaches a sphere, the minimum-area shape; when weight matters, the capillary length controls the deformation.
- **Wetting and the contact angle.** — The equilibrium contact angle is measured through the liquid where three interfaces meet; an angle below 90 degrees indicates preferential wetting but depends on material and surface cleanliness.
- **Capillary rise.** — For a thin circular capillary, h = 2 gamma cos(theta)/(rho g r); the sign of cos(theta) determines a rise or depression.
- **The role of scale: why small organisms interact with water differently.** — The ratio of surface force to weight grows approximately as 1/L^2 as size falls, allowing a water strider to be supported by an interface while the same mechanism cannot support a large animal.

## Short example

- **Problem:** For water, gamma = 0.072 N/m and rho = 1000 kg/m^3; take the contact angle as zero. Find the rise in a capillary of radius 0.50 mm with g = 9.81 m/s^2.
- **Answer:** The ideal model predicts a rise of about 2.9 cm.
- **Check:** The result has units of length and is inversely proportional to radius; a tube half as wide would predict twice the rise.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Elementary formulas assume an equilibrium clean interface, constant gamma, a specified contact angle, and a cylindrical tube; dynamic wetting, evaporation, contamination, and contact-angle hysteresis require additional modeling.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Surface tension is not a literal skin stretched over the liquid: it is a thermodynamic property of an interface, with forces acting along any line drawn in that interface.

## Self-check

1. Why is a small drop closer to spherical than a large drop of the same liquid on a horizontal surface?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Compare the scaling of surface energy and gravitational energy with size.
> At small size the surface contribution is larger relative to weight, so area minimization dominates; gravity flattens a larger drop more strongly.

2. By what factor does capillary height change if tube radius increases from 0.25 to 1.0 mm with all other parameters fixed?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> The formula gives h proportional to 1/r.
> The radius increases by a factor of 4, so the height decreases by a factor of 4.
