# 6.1. States of Matter and the Continuum Approximation

> [!abstract] In brief
> A state of matter is determined by the balance between thermal motion, interactions among particles, and external conditions rather than by the substance name alone. A solid preserves long-range or local structural order, a liquid flows while remaining nearly fixed in volume, a gas fills the available volume, and a plasma contains enough free charges to respond collectively to electromagnetic fields.

## Core explanation

A macroscopic description averages over a huge number of particles inside a small physical region. If its linear size still spans many particles but remains much smaller than the scale over which the flow changes, fields such as $\rho(\mathbf r,t)$, $p(\mathbf r,t)$, and $T(\mathbf r,t)$ can be defined. The continuum approximation becomes unreliable when the mean free path or a microstructural size is comparable to the problem scale.

## Key ideas

- **Solids, liquids, gases, and plasma.** — The states differ through their ability to preserve shape and volume, the character of particle order, and the presence of mobile charge carriers; a phase diagram locates transitions under specified conditions.
- **Density and pressure.** — Density $\rho = dm/dV$ measures mass in a local volume, whereas pressure p is normal force per unit area; in an equilibrium medium both can be assigned at every point.
- **Microscopic structure and macroscopic properties.** — Temperature, pressure, and viscosity are not properties of a single molecule in the same sense as they are properties of a system; they emerge after statistical averaging of microscopic motion and collisions.
- **When matter can be treated as a continuous medium.** — A practical criterion is a small Knudsen number $\mathrm{Kn} = \lambda/L$, where $\lambda$ is the mean free path and L is the characteristic scale of macroscopic variation.

## Short example

- **Problem:** The molecular mean free path in air is 70 nm and the characteristic channel width is 1.0 mm. Estimate the Knudsen number and decide whether ordinary fluid mechanics is suitable away from the walls.
- **Answer:** $\mathrm{Kn} = 7.0 \times 10^{-5}$; the continuum model is appropriate for the bulk flow.
- **Check:** Kn is dimensionless and decreases for a wider channel; a separate boundary condition may still be needed immediately next to a wall.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> A continuum model requires Kn much less than 1 and local thermodynamic equilibrium; a rarefied gas, nanoscopic channel, shock front, or granular medium may require a kinetic or discrete description.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> A plasma is not merely a very hot ideal gas: even a modest ionized fraction can produce collective behavior through long-range fields and introduce new length and time scales.

## Self-check

1. Why cannot pressure be assigned to one selected gas molecule?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Compare one instantaneous molecular impulse with an averaged momentum flux through an area.
> Pressure is the statistical mean normal momentum flux of many particles per unit area; one molecular path supplies only isolated impulses.

2. A gas has $\lambda = 20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in a channel 0.20 mm wide. Find Kn and assess the model.

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Put both lengths in metres and divide $\lambda$ by L.
> $\mathrm{Kn} = 0.10$. This is a transition regime, so conventional continuum fluid mechanics without slip corrections is unreliable.
