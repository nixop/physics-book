# 13.6. Atoms, Molecules, and Spectra

> [!abstract] In brief
> Rutherford scattering showed that positive charge and nearly all atomic mass occupy a tiny nucleus. Bohr's model introduced discrete orbits and reproduced the main hydrogen levels, but modern quantum mechanics replaces electron trajectories with orbitals: states carrying probability distributions.

## Core explanation

Quantum numbers and the Pauli principle organize electron-state filling and underlie periodic chemical behavior. In a molecule, electron states belong to the entire nuclear arrangement; transitions absorb or emit photons with hf = |ΔE|, and stimulated emission permits optical amplification.

## Key ideas

- **Rutherford's experiment and the Bohr model as a historical stepping stone.** — Large-angle alpha-particle scattering is rare but requires a compact massive center. Bohr orbits remain historically useful and give the hydrogen-like spectrum, yet they do not describe general many-electron atoms.
- **Atomic orbitals and quantum numbers.** — An orbital is labeled by spatial quantum numbers n, l, and m and is not a path around the nucleus. Its shape represents a state amplitude, while spin projection supplies another quantum number.
- **The Pauli exclusion principle and the periodic table.** — No two electrons in an atom may have the same complete quantum-number set. Together with orbital energies and electron interactions, this produces shells and recurring valence configurations across the periodic table.
- **Chemical bonding at a qualitative level.** — A covalent bond can be understood through occupation of bonding molecular states that lower total energy. Real bonding includes kinetic energy and attraction and repulsion among all charges; it is not merely a static shared-pair picture.
- **Energy transitions, spectra, and lasers.** — A spectral frequency comes from a level difference, while intensity depends on populations and selection rules. A laser needs a gain medium, a population inversion or equivalent pumping scheme, stimulated emission, and resonator feedback.

## Short example

- **Problem:** Find the photon energy and wavelength for a hydrogen transition from n = 3 to n = 2, using Eₙ = -13.6 eV/n².
- **Answer:** The photon energy is about 1.89 eV and its wavelength about 656 nm.
- **Check:** The transition ends at n = 2 and belongs to the visible Balmer series; 656 nm is red light.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Eₙ = -13.6 eV/n² applies to an isolated nonrelativistic hydrogen-like ion with an infinitely heavy point nucleus. Precision spectroscopy needs reduced mass, fine and hyperfine structure, and radiative corrections; many-electron atoms require electron-interaction methods.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> An orbital is not a blurred classical orbit of one electron. It is a state amplitude; one position measurement gives one point, while a distribution emerges from repeated preparations.

## Self-check

1. How many electrons may occupy one spatial orbital, and how must their states differ?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Apply the Pauli principle to spin projection.
> At most two; their spin projections must be opposite so their complete quantum-number sets differ.

2. Find the photon wavelength for the hydrogen transition n = 2 to n = 1.

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Use ΔE = 13.6 eV·(1 - 1/4), then λ = 1240 eV·nm/ΔE.
> ΔE = 10.2 eV and λ ≈ 121.6 nm, an ultraviolet Lyman-series line.
