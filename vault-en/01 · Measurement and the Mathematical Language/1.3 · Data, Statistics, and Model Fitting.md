# 1.3. Data, Statistics, and Model Fitting

> [!abstract] In brief
> Raw data become a physical result after the procedure is documented, the distribution is visualized, and predictions are compared with observations. A mean describes a center only alongside a measure of spread, while a residual plot can reveal failures hidden by a single goodness metric.

## Core explanation

Model fitting estimates parameters under assumptions about the noise. It does not turn correlation into causation: a causal conclusion also requires a mechanism, temporal ordering, and a design that excludes plausible confounding factors.

## Key ideas

- **A series of measurements, the mean value, and the spread.** — The mean summarizes the level of a series and standard deviation describes scatter of individual observations. Standard uncertainty of the mean decreases approximately as 1/√n only for independent, stable measurements.
- **Histograms and the normal distribution at an intuitive level.** — A histogram depends on bin width and displays an empirical distribution shape. A normal distribution often arises from a sum of small independent contributions, but it is not a universal law for all data.
- **Outliers: when they may be excluded and when they may not.** — An observation should be excluded only by a predefined physical or procedural criterion, such as a confirmed sensor failure. Removing a point merely because it damages the plot biases the result.
- **Straight-line fitting, residuals, and goodness of fit.** — A residual is observation minus prediction. A random cloud consistent with noise supports the model shape, whereas curvature, trend, or changing width indicates an omitted dependence or an unsuitable noise model.
- **Correlation does not necessarily imply causation.** — Two quantities can move together because of a third factor, a shared trend, or data selection. Even a strong correlation coefficient does not establish the direction of causation.

## Short example

- **Problem:** For x = 0, 1, 2, 3, observations are y = 1.1, 3.0, 4.9, 7.2. Is the trial model y = 1 + 2x adequate at a precision scale of about 0.2?
- **Answer:** All residuals are no larger than 0.2 and show no obvious trend, so the data are compatible with the trial line at this precision, but do not identify it as the only possible model.
- **Check:** The mean residual is 0.05, small on the 0.2 scale; this does not replace evaluation of parameter uncertainties.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> Ordinary linear regression assumes a correct model form and a suitable description of errors; outliers, unequal variance, uncertainty in x, and temporal autocorrelation require another method or explicit modeling.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> A high R² does not prove a physical law: a common time trend can produce it, while a systematic residual pattern still exposes model failure.

## Self-check

1. One point is far from all others, but the notebook records no malfunction. May it be deleted before fitting?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Distinguish statistical surprise from an established cause of error.
> Not automatically. Inspect the primary record, test robustness with and without the point, apply a justified predeclared criterion, and report the decision transparently.

2. Find the mean of 4.8, 5.0, 5.2, and 5.0, then sum their deviations from the mean.

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Add the four values first; deviations from an arithmetic mean have a special property.
> The mean is 5.0. The deviations −0.2, 0, +0.2, and 0 sum to zero, as they must for an arithmetic mean.
