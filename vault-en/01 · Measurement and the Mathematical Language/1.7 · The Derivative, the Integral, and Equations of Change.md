# 1.7. The Derivative, the Integral, and Equations of Change

> [!abstract] In brief
> A derivative is the limiting ratio of a small change in a quantity to a small change in its argument. An integral performs the inverse accumulation: it sums infinitesimal contributions and needs an initial value to reconstruct a particular history.

## Core explanation

Most dynamical laws specify a rate of change rather than a finished trajectory. A differential equation plus initial and boundary conditions defines evolution, while a numerical method approximates it with finite steps whose error must be controlled.

## Key ideas

- **The derivative as the instantaneous rate of change.** — An average rate refers to a finite interval, while a derivative belongs to an instant. On a graph it is the tangent slope and has the unit of the dependent quantity divided by the unit of its argument.
- **The integral as an accumulated quantity.** — A definite integral includes the sign of contributions and is geometrically a signed area. For example, integrating power over time gives transferred energy.
- **The connection between finite increments and continuous change.** — A finite increment equals the integral of the instantaneous rate. Over a small step it can be approximated by derivative times step, but repeated local errors accumulate.
- **A differential equation as a rule governing the evolution of a system.** — A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives. The same rule can produce different solutions from different initial states, so the equation alone is insufficient.
- **Numerical solution in small steps.** — Euler's method replaces a curve by successive tangent steps. A smaller step often improves accuracy, but stability and convergence rate depend on both the equation and method.

## Short example

- **Problem:** A body moves along an axis with a(t) = −2t m/s², v(0) = 10 m/s, and x(0) = 0. Find its velocity and displacement at t = 3 s.
- **Answer:** After 3 s the velocity is 1 m/s and displacement is 21 m in the positive direction.
- **Check:** Velocity stays positive but decreases from 10 to 1 m/s, so displacement must lie between 3 and 30 m; 21 m passes this scale check.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> A derivative requires a defined limiting process, and simple time stepping can become unstable; numerical results should be checked by reducing the step, monitoring conservation laws, and comparing known limits.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Integrating a derivative gives a family of functions: the missing constant is fixed by an initial condition and can completely change the physical solution.

## Self-check

1. Why does dx/dt = 2 not uniquely specify x(t)?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> What information about a constant offset disappears upon differentiation?
> Every function x(t) = 2t + C has the same derivative. An initial condition such as x(0) is needed to determine C.

2. For dv/dt = −0.5v and v₀ = 10 m/s, perform one explicit Euler step of length 0.2 s.

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Use v₁ = v₀ + Δt(−0.5v₀).
> v₁ = 10 + 0.2×(−5) = 9.0 m/s. The exact value 10e⁻⁰·¹ ≈ 9.05 m/s displays the finite-step error.
