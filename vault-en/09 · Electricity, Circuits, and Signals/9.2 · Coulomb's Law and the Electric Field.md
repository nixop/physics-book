# 9.2. Coulomb's Law and the Electric Field

> [!abstract] In brief
> Coulomb's law gives the vacuum force between stationary point charges: magnitude falls as 1/r^2 and direction lies along their separation. Vector form automatically handles signs and direction. With several sources, forces and fields add vectorially by superposition.

## Core explanation

Electric field E is force per unit positive test charge in the limit that the probe does not disturb the sources: F = q_test E. Field lines encode only tangent direction and qualitative density; their count and placement are chosen by the illustrator, the lines do not move, and they cannot cross where field direction is unique.

## Key ideas

- **The interaction between point charges.** — A point source q produces E = k q r_vector/r^3; the singularity at r = 0 marks an idealization limit rather than an infinite measurable field inside a real extended body.
- **The principle of superposition.** — Each source contribution is evaluated independently in an unchanged linear medium and components are summed; magnitudes can be added only for aligned vectors.
- **Electric field strength.** — E has units N/C or V/m; field depends on sources and position, whereas force also depends on the probe charge.
- **Field lines and their limitations as a visualization.** — Denser drawn lines conventionally indicate stronger field, but the drawing does not define exact magnitude or literal connections between charges.

## Short example

- **Problem:** Charges +2.0 nC and -2.0 nC lie on the x-axis at -0.10 m and +0.10 m. Find the field at the origin; k = 8.99e9 N m^2/C^2.
- **Answer:** E = 3.60 kN/C along +x, from the positive charge toward the negative one.
- **Check:** The contributions reinforce rather than cancel because both arrows point the same way at the midpoint.

> [!warning] Limits of the model
> The direct Coulomb formula applies to stationary point charges in vacuum; extended charge requires integration, matter requires polarization, and rapidly changing sources require retarded electromagnetic fields.

> [!danger] Common mistake
> Zero net charge does not imply zero field everywhere: a dipole's far field falls faster than a monopole field but remains nonzero.

## Self-check

1. Can two field lines cross where E is nonzero?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> A vector at one point has one direction.
> No. A crossing would assign two tangent directions to one vector; at E = 0 the direction is simply undefined.

2. What is the field magnitude 0.30 m from a +1.0 nC point charge in vacuum?

> [!tip]- Answer and guide
> Use E = kq/r^2.
> E = 8.99e9 x 1.0e-9 / 0.30^2, approximately 100 N/C.
